# Fictional vehicle design: IP and risk framework

## Purpose and limits

Planetary Engine needs a large, coherent vehicle catalogue without paying for licences to reproduce real manufacturers' cars. The production target is original fictional vehicles informed by broad automotive design history and engineering conventions.

This document is research guidance, not a legal opinion or a guarantee of non-infringement. The analysis below focuses on the EU/Czech Republic and the United States. Distribution in other territories can introduce additional rights and tests. Before commercial release, obtain jurisdiction-specific review for unusually close designs, prominent real-world references, or disputed assets.

**Core rule:** create a new vehicle design, not a renamed replica. A fictional badge does not neutralize copying of protected appearance; changing a percentage of geometry is not a legal safe harbour.

## Rights that must be considered separately

### 1. Copyright

Czech Copyright Act No. 121/2000 Coll. protects an original work that is a unique result of the author's creative activity and is expressed in an objectively perceptible form (§ 2). The Act also makes clear that an idea, method, principle or procedure as such is not a work. A particular expressive design may be protected; broad ideas such as "mid-engine supercar", "1970s rally coupe", "boxy utility SUV" or ordinary engineering solutions are not automatically monopolized as such.

At EU level, CJEU case law describes a work as the author's own intellectual creation reflecting free and creative choices. In *Brompton Bicycle* (C-833/18), technical constraints that leave no room for creative freedom do not create copyright originality; technical function alone is not protected as expression. This does **not** mean that every functional product shape is automatically free to copy: copyright, design rights and trade marks have separate tests.

Practical implication:
- Use vehicle categories, engineering principles and broad era/style references as creative inputs.
- Do not trace, reproduce or closely reconstruct expressive bodywork, signature light graphics, distinctive ornamental details or a complete recognizable design.
- Do not assume that a car is unprotected merely because it is a functional object.
- Treat photos, scans, CAD files, game models and manufacturer press images as separately protected source material; access to a reference is not a licence to reproduce it.

### 2. Registered and unregistered design rights in the EU

As of 19 November 2026, the current codified EU instrument is Regulation (EU) 2026/715 on European Union designs, in force from 1 July 2026. It codifies the EU design regime after the 2024 reform. The Czech national framework includes Act No. 207/2000 Coll. on the Protection of Industrial Designs.

A design concerns the appearance of all or part of a product, including lines, contours, colours, shape, texture and materials. EU design protection is based on novelty and individual character. The assessment of individual character compares the overall impression on the informed user, taking account of the designer's degree of freedom. This is not a simple count of changed parts.

Registered EU designs can be renewed in five-year periods up to 25 years. Unregistered EU design protection is shorter (three years from qualifying first public disclosure in the EU) and, unlike registered design protection, is directed at copying: independent creation is an important distinction. Verify the current Regulation's exact provisions for the relevant use and date.

For the Czech registered national design, the Industrial Property Office explains the same central concepts: appearance, novelty, individual character, overall impression and designer freedom; national registration may last up to 25 years through renewals.

Practical implication:
- Do not use one reference car as a template and merely alter small details.
- Compare the complete vehicle appearance, especially front, side, rear, roof and three-quarter views.
- Treat signature proportions and combinations of visible features as potential risk indicators, not just individual parts.
- Search registered designs and relevant prior art when a design remains close to a particular production car.
- Keep evidence of independent design development and source choices.

### 3. Trade marks and three-dimensional marks

EU trade marks can consist of words, graphics, colours, sounds and shapes of goods. Regulation (EU) 2017/1001 includes exclusions for signs consisting exclusively of a shape resulting from the nature of the goods, necessary to obtain a technical result, or giving substantial value to the goods. These exclusions do not mean that all vehicle shapes are unprotectable. Distinctive badges, logos, model names, grille emblems, wheel centre caps and other source-identifying signs require separate review.

Practical implication:
- Invent manufacturer names, model names, badges, emblems, typography and dealership identities.
- Do not use real manufacturer marks in geometry, textures, UI, filenames intended for public release, marketing renders or screenshots.
- Avoid confusingly similar names and logos, not merely exact copies.
- Do not imply sponsorship, endorsement or official affiliation.

### 4. US trade dress

In the United States, product design may be protected as trade dress under the Lanham Act where the legal requirements are met. In *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc.*, 529 U.S. 205 (2000), the Supreme Court held that product design is not inherently distinctive for this purpose; secondary meaning is required. *TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc.*, 532 U.S. 23 (2001), explains the functionality limitation and cautions against extending trade dress to functional product features.

This is not a blanket permission to imitate cars. The analysis can involve source identification, confusion, non-functionality, acquired distinctiveness and other facts. Registered design patents and other rights may also be relevant in the US.

Practical implication:
- Do not copy a complete distinctive product appearance and rely on the absence of a logo.
- Do not assume that common functional features are protected, or that every non-functional feature is free to copy.
- Avoid a design that makes players reasonably identify the fictional car as a particular manufacturer's product.

## What is not a safe rule

The following are not reliable legal tests:
- "Change 20/30/40 percent of the mesh."
- "Use two or three donor cars and it becomes original."
- "Change the badge and grille."
- "Make the car fictional and it is automatically safe."
- "Only the logo is protected."
- "The competitor did it, so we can do it."
- "The reference is visible online, therefore it is free to use."

A multi-source mashup is a useful creative technique, not a legal defence by itself. A mashup can still reproduce a protected, recognizable overall impression or several protected signature elements.

## Planetary Engine risk policy

### Green — preferred

- Start from vehicle class, era, engineering layout, market segment and use case.
- Use multiple broad references for different design problems (e.g. packaging, roofline, stance, material language), not a single donor vehicle.
- Build original proportions, silhouette, glazing, light graphics, surface breaks and detailing.
- Use fictional brands and an internally consistent design language.
- Preserve sketches, parameter histories, source logs and authorship/provenance.
- Search for confusingly similar designs before accepting a hero vehicle.

### Amber — manual review

- A design is repeatedly described by reviewers as "basically a [specific make/model]".
- One real vehicle dominates the side silhouette or front/rear identity.
- The design repeats a distinctive combination of lamps, grille, side intakes, greenhouse and body surfacing.
- A real-world brand or model name appears in prompts, metadata or promotional material.
- A model was generated from one exact reference image or a traced outline.
- The asset is intended for marketing, close-up hero use or commercial merchandising.

### Red — reject or redesign

- Exact or near-exact body scan, CAD model, manufacturer model or game asset without a valid licence.
- Real badges, logos, model names or protected graphic marks copied into the asset.
- Direct tracing of a production car's complete silhouette and signature surfaces.
- A deliberately disguised copy whose only material changes are the badge, name, grille or minor trim.
- A source file or texture whose provenance/licence cannot be established.

## Required asset record

Every vehicle must store:
- fictional manufacturer and model IDs;
- class, era, intended market and engineering layout;
- author/agent and generation tool/version;
- prompt and design brief version;
- references consulted, source URLs, access dates and licence/usage notes;
- original sketches/blockouts and key design decisions;
- similarity review status and reviewer;
- any legal escalation or approval record;
- Blender file hash, exported asset hash and revision.

Do not store a real manufacturer/model as the fictional vehicle's identity. If references are recorded for internal provenance, mark them as inspiration/reference only, not as source geometry or a claim of affiliation.

## Review checklist

1. Are all marks and names fictional?
2. Is the overall silhouette original when viewed from front, side, rear and three-quarter angles?
3. Does the front/rear identity rely on a distinctive copied light/grille treatment?
4. Is one specific production vehicle still immediately identifiable?
5. Were source models, photographs or CAD files copied or traced?
6. Are the reference assets licensed for the actual use?
7. Is the design history documented?
8. Has a human reviewer recorded a reasoned outcome?
9. Has legal review been requested where risk remains material?

A pass on this checklist is an internal production gate, not a legal certificate.
